# THE HUMAN WALL

**This is why I do this work.** I began by examining my own reasoning and corrections. I keep asking whether growing up with the enormous scale of the universe — and everything we know we don't know — changes how we approach the smaller problem actually in front of us.

Sometimes we may carry uncertainty about *everything* into a question where the relevant evidence is much closer. We expand the possibilities, fear being wrong, or distrust what the local observation supports. When this becomes normal, the same problem may recur in language, methods, evaluation, and the systems we build. This is my research question, not a proven description of everybody.

**I am investigating that human wall.** The finite mathematics, interactive worlds, ARC work, harnesses, AI-assisted software, and research records are instruments for investigating and correcting relationships. They are not separate explanations of what the project means to me.

We grow up hearing how far away the stars are, how old the universe is, how much there is beyond what we can see, and how much may remain beyond what anyone can explain. None of that is small. None of it needs to be made small. But what happens when we take the scale of all that uncertainty with us into an ordinary question? What happens when the fact that we cannot know *everything* begins to feel like a reason we cannot know enough about *this*?

A lightbulb doesn't turn on. We could think about every failure a lightbulb has ever had, every possibility hidden in the electrical system, every exception to every explanation. Or we could begin with the bulb, the switch, the power, the connection. We might be wrong. The first check might fail. Something else might turn out to matter. But a mistake at that scale gives us another observation. It doesn't make the whole universe a condition we have to satisfy before we can move.

Now move that question out of the room. Into a conversation. Someone says something and we hear something slightly different. A word seems familiar, so we attach it to the thing we already recognize. The next sentence makes less sense, but rather than returning to what was said, we explain the misunderstanding with more words. The explanation grows. It can become convincing. Eventually we may be defending a description of something we stopped following several sentences ago. How much of that confidence came from observation, and how much came from the explanation becoming familiar?

It isn't only certainty that can carry us away. Uncertainty can do it too. We can keep widening the question because somewhere outside the things we've checked there might be something we missed. There often *is* something we missed. The question is whether that possibility belongs to the problem at hand, and what would actually bring it into view. If the unknown is allowed to stand for every missing observation, then even a result we can inspect may never feel sufficient. If a confident description is allowed to stand for the result instead, we can stop checking too soon. Those movements look different, but both can leave us relating to an imagined problem in place of the one in front of us.

I wonder how often this becomes ordinary. Not because someone deliberately chooses to make a simple thing complicated, and not because caution, imagination, or asking for help is wrong. I mean the quieter habit of expanding before looking, doubting what has been observed because of what hasn't, or treating the number of possible explanations as if it determines how much evidence is already present. Once a habit is ordinary, it can travel into how we speak, how we teach, how we make rules, how we test software, and how we decide whether another person's answer counts. The movement may be familiar enough that we stop noticing it.

But noticing it doesn't put me outside it. I can do the same thing while trying to explain it. I can write a sentence that sounds precise and still follows the wrong relationship. Someone can correct me, and I can answer their correction with another polished version of the same mistake. The important part isn't to declare that I've escaped the wall. It's to return to what moved, what was actually said, what I added, what survived the correction, and what still hasn't been observed. If the detour gave us something useful, why throw that away? If it gave us a false connection, why keep it just because we spent time getting there?

Now bring money into it. Not as a theory about money, but as one more condition I have to work inside. I have a family to take care of, and I can't throw a funded team at each problem. Sometimes I look at the workstation and think, "If I had actual agents this wouldn't look so jank." I might be right about part of that. More resources would change what I could build and how quickly I could check it. But notice what happens if I let that sentence stand in for the investigation. Have I found a missing capability, or have I stopped checking which specific handoff lost the question? Is the problem the tool I don't have, the source I never carried, or the result I haven't tested yet? Those are different walls, even if they feel like one enormous thing when I need the work to move.

Suppose we open every pipe and leave the whole operation running until something announces itself finished. One worker passes a description to another, another changes it, another makes a blueprint, and eventually there is something that looks ready. Now give that blueprint to a game world and ask what it actually does. What if the return is different? "What the fuck happened? Call Bob!" All right. What does Bob get? The original source? The exact boundary? The handoff where the relationship changed? Or just a confident summary of everything that was supposed to have happened? If we haven't kept those things present, asking more workers to continue can make the explanation bigger while the problem itself stays exactly where we lost it.

That's part of what I'm investigating with bounded workers, source records, private workstation copies, and explicit handoffs. I want the next movement to carry what the task actually depends on and return something another check can challenge. I want a blueprint to come back as an inspectable record, not as a promise that the physical world will agree with it. A game-world test only verifies what its defined construction can test; an unexpected native return isn't something to smooth over because the workflow was green. Keep the unexpected result. Keep the source it contradicted. Keep the part we don't know. Then ask what actually moved. The next correction might be small. It might expose a missing relationship in the construction. Either way, not knowing everything hasn't stopped us from examining what is right here.

This is one reason I work with finite, bounded constructions. A few defined movements can produce complicated behavior through repetition and interaction. Their results don't require us to list every possible story about them in advance; they require us to follow what the construction actually permits, and to correct a description when the returned state doesn't match it. That doesn't prove how the universe works or how everyone thinks. It gives me a way to ask narrower questions without pretending the rest of reality has disappeared.

The same care matters when I move between mathematics, language, games, physical questions, and software. They aren't automatically the same thing because a relationship reminds me of another one. A conversation has someone's actual words and corrections. A game has permitted actions and returned states. A mathematical construction has its defined domain and operators. A program has source, execution, and results that may or may not agree with its description. I can carry a question across them. I can't carry a proof from one into another just because I like the way it sounds.

So how much do we really need to know to begin? Not *nothing*. Not *everything*. We need to find what the next movement depends on. We need to be willing to notice when what comes back is different from what we expected. We need to let an observation change our explanation without making the correction into another story we refuse to question. And maybe, while reading this, you've already had to decide whether some of these sentences describe something you've done, something someone else has done, or something you don't agree with at all. That difference is worth keeping. The question was never supposed to make everybody arrive at the same answer.

I started with my own misunderstandings because those were there to examine. The work got larger as I followed them into other subjects, but its beginning wasn't a machine, a theory of everyone else, or a claim that the unknown has been solved. It was a person trying to keep hold of a relationship while the words around it kept changing.

### Evidence and verification

The standard is source-first: identify the native referent, record an intervention and its returned result, compare against a prediction, and retain counterexamples. A passing test verifies its stated boundary; it does not by itself demonstrate a general solution, a physical measurement, or a successful external deployment.

**Selected source-pinned evidence, last documented October 20, 2026:** the original SP80 SDK advanced to **level 3 of 6** on reproduced native histories, while [32 subsequent stage-3 trials](https://github.com/huntingthereferent/autobots-assemble/actions/runs/39471887855) did not reach level 4. The NIH archive comparison recorded **1,345 differences in status fields** across two source datasets, without establishing the extraction chronology. A/B WORLD records conditional model results, not measured hardware collisions. These are separate results with separate limits, not one combined proof.

### Research method

I work from small, bounded questions toward explicit tests: original source → predicted change → permitted action or transformation → returned record → independent comparison. When the evidence fails, the failed prediction remains part of the investigation. Mathematical correspondence, executable behavior, natural-language interpretation, and physical measurement each require their own grounds.
