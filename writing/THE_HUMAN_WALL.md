# The Human Wall

*The root question behind Hunting the Referent — how our sense of the scale of reality may shape the way we approach the problem in front of us.*

[← Profile](../README.md) · [Writing](README.md) · [Origin](ORIGIN.md)

## Where I started

I've spent a lot of time trying to understand how I solve problems, including what happens when I misunderstand something, how I correct myself, and how easily an explanation can move away from the thing I was originally looking at.

I didn't start by constructing a theory about other people. I started by observing myself. I wanted to know what I was actually doing while I was thinking, not just how I described it afterward. That meant paying attention to mistakes, revisiting my own assumptions, and sometimes finding that the problem I was trying to solve wasn't the problem in front of me anymore.

This is the reason for my research. I did not begin with a plan to build an AI system, a mathematical framework, or a workstation. Those came into the investigation later. **The Human Wall is the question behind them, not another name for the tools.**

## The scale we bring to a problem

We grow up learning about an unimaginably large universe. We're introduced to infinity, enormous distances, complexity, and things we may never fully understand. It's natural to look at something that large and expect the explanation to be equally enormous. I wonder how much of that expectation we carry into ordinary problem-solving.

We consider possibilities, then possibilities within those possibilities. We anticipate mistakes, construct explanations, correct those explanations, and sometimes build entire systems around things that haven't happened. Much of this can come from trying to understand something properly and not wanting to be wrong. I recognize this behavior because I've observed it in myself.

The difficulty I'm calling **the Human Wall** is not simply that our brains have limits or that we sometimes misunderstand one another. It's the possibility that our relationship to the size of reality changes our relationship to an immediate, answerable problem. We can treat *not knowing everything* as if it means *not knowing enough about this particular thing*. That can make a limited question feel limitless.

Take a lightbulb that doesn't turn on. I don't need an explanation of the whole universe to begin checking the bulb, the switch, the power, or the connection. The relevant conditions have to be discovered, not assumed; but possibilities outside those conditions shouldn't automatically decide what I can or cannot know here.

The size of reality has not changed. **What I need to investigate has.**

## Why I call it a wall

I don't think the interesting part is just an isolated moment of hesitation. I wonder what happens when this way of approaching problems becomes ordinary.

Caution can be useful. So can admitting uncertainty, asking someone for help, and checking the same result again. But those actions can also hide a mismatch: a person may continue questioning because of how enormous *everything else* feels, even after the local evidence answers the actual question. An explanation may sound more thorough because it contains more possibilities, even when those possibilities don't belong to the observed problem.

And the pattern may recur across layers. At the immediate problem, it can appear as disproportionate doubt. In a description, it can become speculation that drifts away from what happened. In a method, it can produce unnecessary conditions or checks. In evaluation, it can make people distrust adequate evidence or accept an answer for its authority instead of its support. In a group or institution, similar habits may be rewarded as normal seriousness.

I use *recurring across layers* deliberately. I'm investigating whether a relationship appears again in different settings, not claiming I've proved a literal mathematical fractal of human behavior. Nor am I claiming that everyone does this, or that other people have the private motives I might imagine for them. My starting evidence is what I can observe and correct, including in my own work.

The most difficult possibility is that the habits sustaining the wall can look reasonable from inside them. That is what I want people to notice.

## A different place to begin

Basic mathematics offers me one useful distinction: **the complexity of what a system produces doesn't necessarily tell us how complicated its starting conditions must be.**

A small finite set of relationships can produce complicated patterns through repetition and interaction. A process can continue without requiring a new foundational rule for every result. This does not say that the physical universe is finite, that all problems are simple, or that every relationship is already known. It gives me a reason not to start by inventing every possibility.

Instead, I can return to what's actually present, follow a movement, compare what came back, and correct the relationship when the description no longer fits.

This is where A/B comes into my work. I'm interested in what changes between states, what remains related, and what survives further movement. I don't use a local observation as a reason to erase all other angles or declare the rest of reality irrelevant. I use what is in front of me to investigate without forcing an answer from what I imagine might be there.

The mathematics makes particular relationships executable and challengeable. I've been constructing bounded systems, including deliberately constrained oscillation and interacting movements, to see what actually follows. The early nine-anchor and later 48-law experiments are different working constructions, not established fundamental counts or a universal minimum. The [three-coordinate finite-field model](https://github.com/huntingthereferent/autobots-assemble/blob/main/work/arc_like_maze/math.py) and [two-predecessor/window work](https://github.com/huntingthereferent/autobots-assemble/blob/main/work/arc_like_maze/WINDOW_COMBINED.md) have their own definitions, tests, and limits.

## Why the repositories contain so many different things

Language, mathematics, interactive worlds, ARC investigations, native execution tests, and the workstation may look like separate ambitions. To me, they're ways to investigate one underlying concern: **when do we stop relating to what actually supports a problem and start carrying a larger, imagined problem in its place?**

I use harnesses and software to preserve the observations, attempted movements, failures, corrections, and independent checks. I don't want to reconstruct the whole path every time I return to a question. A successful test tells me what passed in that specific system. It doesn't make my interpretation of the Human Wall true.

Language matters for the same reason. Descriptions travel between people and systems. If an interpretation stops matching what supports it, repeating that interpretation can carry the error further. I want to preserve both the original expression and what changed in the correction rather than polish away the mismatch.

These are instruments and research records. **They are not the root problem I am trying to address.** And I don't need everyone to use A/B, my software, or my mathematical constructions to recognize and investigate the problem themselves.

## What is still a question

I have an account of where this question came from and some observations that motivate it. I have **not** demonstrated that learning about the universe's scale causes this behavior in general, or that the same mechanism explains every example of human hesitation.

One way to test part of it would be to hold a practical problem and its evidence constant while varying how its scope or uncertainty is framed. Does the person begin considering irrelevant possibilities, defer an adequately supported answer, or change what they count as sufficient evidence? Those outcomes need to be measured, and there may be explanations other than the one I'm proposing.

That is why correction is part of the work. A failed explanation is something to investigate, not permission to declare the next one true.

I want to understand how much we can improve our relationship to the problem in front of us without pretending we can know everything. The question I keep returning to is:

**What is actually here, what changes when something moves, and what can I carry forward without losing the relationship?**
