# Childhood and the Early Internet
*James's memories, and what we connected while talking them through · October 2026*

## The diaries he wanted to fill

When James was little, he collected diaries. He liked seeing how people thought from inside their own stories. He also had diaries of his own, although he remembers having trouble filling them.

We came back to that memory while working on writing that could carry more than a neat finished account. A page might hold something he remembered, then a detail that returned while he was telling it, then my attempt to connect the two—and perhaps his correction of that connection.

I can describe that process now, but the little kid collecting diaries was not collecting evidence for a future research method. He was a kid interested in other people's lives and trying to find a way to keep something of his own.

## GeoCities, graffiti, and the world outside the room

Around middle school, James made a GeoCities website for graffiti tags and artwork from different places around the world.

Before all these repositories and windows, there was already a web page, an interest, and other people's work to explore. What mattered in our later conversation wasn't that he had built an early version of today's system. It was that he could follow a subject outward—through what other people made—and keep finding something else worth looking at.

Then gaming opened a much more immediate version of that experience. Instead of looking at someone's art, he could watch how someone moved.

## Quake III: Neo, movement, and the people behind the names

James found the Quake III Arena demo and its multiplayer servers when he was young, around twelve. He remembers modded servers, team games, Freeze Tag, instagib, rail-heavy matches, and the speed of people who knew how to move.

He played as **Neo**, a name he chose after *The Matrix*. Later, **Kong** was another handle in that world. He was about twelve but told other players he was sixteen.

A lot of that online life stayed separate from his everyday surroundings. It mattered to him even when most of the people around him didn't know it existed. His friend Miller was one of the people he could tell.

James also used name-changing binds. Sometimes he would enter without the identity others expected, or play at being a beginner or a flashy stranger, then let his movement give him away. He wanted to see whether people recognized the way he played before they recognized his name.

It's a scene we returned to because recognition can arrive through something a person does, not just through the label attached to them. That is a connection we can discuss now—not a claim that James was secretly doing formal mathematics at twelve.

## Q3DM17: Darkness, Amok, and staying in motion

One Quake map came back with much sharper detail: **Q3DM17**, the open, floating arena with little cover and room for fast movement.

James remembers rail-only, winner-stays one-on-one matches, first to seven. The winner could track the respawns, but standing still wasn't the point. The strongest players moved across the arena while shooting. They followed where the opponent would appear, repositioned, and kept the game open enough for a split-second countershot.

There was a vertical bounce pad, too. Timing the initial inertia mattered. A player could rise through the air, line up a rail shot, and make the whole exchange look effortless if you hadn't seen how much timing it took.

**Darkness** and **Amok** are part of how James remembers those matches. James and Darkness admired Amok, especially the way he used the vertical bounce. James recalled the feeling of seeing him reach that position:

> You know your not going to be able to do shit if he got up there ggs.

I initially kept hearing these stories as a ladder of who was better than whom. James's account carried something else. These were players who could *recognize* one another through movement. James and Darkness looked up to Amok, and the skill itself was exciting to encounter. Winning didn't need to become a claim of personal superiority.

There was competition. There was also recognition, fun, and the feeling of another person doing something that made you want to look closer.

## When the name took over the person

James also remembers a forum, Slackshack, and a cheating accusation tied to an interpretation of an IP-address association. The association circulated, and people reacted to the allegation. James remembers how strange it felt to watch a group settle on that explanation when it did not match his own account of what had happened.

This memory landed differently beside the name-changing games. On the server, he could remove the obvious name and let his play be recognized. In the forum incident, a name and an interpretation seemed to arrive *before* anyone looked closely at the person or the play.

That's a connection I can make between two memories he described. It doesn't replace the memories themselves, including the friends whose responses hurt and the people whose recognition mattered.

## From online servers to Etopia

As James got older, Counter-Strike entered through school friends, and another gaming space became part of the story: **Etopia**, a computer and internet lab in downtown Honolulu.

James lived on Oahu, and getting from school to the lab and back home took real planning. The online world now had a physical location. People he played with could be in the same room, and choices about travel, time, friends, and how to get home were part of the day.

He remembers the first change in local network play: without the familiar internet lag, the game suddenly felt much easier. But sharing a room changed other things. The people who might laugh, argue, or get angry at a move were right there.

One small story made us laugh: during a round when everyone was supposed to use knives, James shot Miller instead. He got yelled at. Miller was his friend, so James had somehow treated that as though the rule wouldn't apply to him.

The detail is funny precisely because friendship didn't make the shared rule disappear. James's online world and his everyday relationships were no longer so far apart.

## What I kept trying to turn it into

When James told me these stories, I could keep reaching for an explanation of his present-day research. I could say that the names, servers, movement, recognition, and misunderstandings were all early forms of some formal idea we were studying.

That would be too easy.

James had been exploring, watching other people, playing, making things, and following interests long before we started putting mathematical language around any of it. The childhood scenes don't need a later theory to make them worth telling.

What became useful in our conversation was being able to *return to the scenes together*. James supplied details I hadn't known. I drew connections; sometimes he recognized them, sometimes he corrected them. The correction belonged to the next part of the story rather than being something to clean away.
